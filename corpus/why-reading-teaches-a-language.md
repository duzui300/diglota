# Why Reading Teaches a Language Better Than Study Does

Most people who learn a language as an adult begin with lists. They memorise cien palabras, luego doscientas, y **se dan cuenta de** (*they realize*) que the lists do not survive contact with a real sentence. A word learned in isolation has no company. Llega sin voz, sin ritmo ni razón (*It arrives without a voice, rhythm, or reason*), and the memory of it fades the way a name fades at a party.

Reading is different because pone cada palabra en el medio de algo (*it puts every word in the middle of something*). **A menudo** a reader **vuelve a** encontrar la misma palabra una y otra vez (*often a reader meets the same word again and again*), each time in a slightly different place, and the meaning is carried by the sentence around it. Nobody has to explain that a word means "however" (**sin embargo**) when the paragraph has already disagreed with itself twice.

**Por lo tanto**, la manera más fácil de leer un idioma extranjero (*the easiest way to read a foreign language*) is to read a text that is almost in your own. One page with un puñado de palabras desconocidas (*a handful of unfamiliar words*) teaches more than ten pages that are entirely unknown, and it teaches them en contexto (*in context*), which is where they will be needed. La dificultad no es el enemigo. La falta de familiaridad sin apoyo lo es, **en vez de** la dificultad (*Unfamiliarity without support is, instead of difficulty*).

The practical consequence es pequeña y poco glamorosa (*is small and unglamorous*). Read something slightly too hard, **a menudo** (*often*), and deja que (*let*) the words you already know carry you past the ones you do not. The vocabulary will follow, porque siempre **vuelve a** hacerlo (*because it always does it again*) when it arrives in company.

**Grammar Breakdown**

1. **darse cuenta de + que:**
- *Example:* "They memorise cien palabras, luego doscientas, y se dan cuenta de (*they realize*) que the lists do not survive contact with a real sentence."
- *Explanation:* Darse cuenta de is a pronominal verb phrase meaning 'to realize.' The reflexive pronoun (me, te, se, nos, os, se) must match the subject, and de introduces what is realized—either a noun or a que clause. In the lesson, the subject is 'they,' so the form is se dan cuenta de que. Learners often drop the reflexive se or the preposition de, saying '*dan cuenta que' or '*se dan cuenta que'; standard Spanish requires both se and de que. Avoid using realizar for 'to realize' in this sense, because realizar normally means 'to carry out.'

2. **volver a + infinitive:**
- *Example:* "A menudo a reader vuelve a encontrar la misma palabra una y otra vez (*often a reader meets the same word again and again*)."
- *Explanation:* Volver a + infinitive is a verbal periphrasis that means 'to do something again.' Here volver does not mean 'to return'; it only marks repetition of the action named by the infinitive. The lesson uses vuelve a encontrar ('meets/finds again') and vuelve a hacerlo ('does it again'). Learners often translate volver literally as 'return' or add otra vez unnecessarily, producing '*vuelve a encontrar otra vez,' which is redundant.

3. **en vez de + noun/infinitive:**
- *Example:* "La falta de familiaridad sin apoyo lo es, en vez de la dificultad (*Unfamiliarity without support is, instead of difficulty*)."
- *Explanation:* En vez de is a prepositional phrase meaning 'instead of.' It introduces the rejected alternative and can be followed by a noun, pronoun, or infinitive—not by a conjugated verb. In the lesson, it contrasts la falta de familiaridad sin apoyo with la dificultad. Learners often say '*en vez que' or attach a conjugated verb directly after de; the correct clause alternative is en lugar de que + subjunctive, not en vez de que in most contexts. Also, do not omit the de.
